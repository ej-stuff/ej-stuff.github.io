Death Habits — and a Correction

Last read flagged that you die in the exact same second as each other in half your losses, and guessed it was proximity — one grenade, one lucky burst, taking both of you out at once. With more games and a closer look at the actual event log, that guess was wrong. Here's what's really happening.

Landing isn't the problem

Only 8 of 213 games (3.8%) end inside the first 90 seconds — that's actually improved since the last read. Whatever trouble finds you, it still mostly finds you later, once the real game has started.

The correction: it's not one bullet killing two people — it's PUBG's knockdown rule. When your last living teammate is eliminated, any teammate who's currently knocked down is auto-eliminated in that same instant, regardless of how they'd otherwise have died. Pulled the full event log for every "died together" loss and checked: in 84 of the 107 (78.5%), one of you had been knocked earlier in that exact fight and never got revived — you weren't hit by the same shot, you were already out of the fight, and the moment the other went down, the game closed out both eliminations on the same clock tick. Zero of the 107 have both of you knocked-and-unrevived at once, which rules out "one grenade got us both" as the driver.

The remaining 23 games (21.5%) don't fit this pattern cleanly — both of you had been revived at some point earlier in the fight, then died close together later in the same push. Those look more like genuine close-quarters double-deaths, but they're the minority case, not the typical one.

What this actually changes about the advice: spacing out doesn't fix this — the knockdown-linked cases aren't a proximity problem. The real lever is what happens in the gap between "teammate goes down" and "the fight is lost": either the revive needs to happen faster (median response is already a strong 25.7s, so this is close to its ceiling), or the standing teammate needs to recognize a 1-vs-many fight early and disengage as a pair rather than trying to hold the position solo while the downed partner waits. A failed solo stand doesn't just cost one life here — it costs both, on the same tick.

Coach's Notes

Five things the data actually supports — refreshed for 213 games, including one correction from last time.

  1. The "simultaneous death" isn't proximity — it's the knock

    78.5% of your together-deaths trace back to one of you already being knocked and unrevived when the other lost the fight. The fix isn't spreading out; it's deciding faster whether to commit to the revive or disengage as a pair before the fight goes to 1-vs-many.

  2. Let fights come to your sightline, not the other way around

    SLR (17.0%), Mini 14 (17.4%), SKS (20.0%), Dragunov (24.3%) — your best accuracy numbers are all single-fire guns used at range, and that pattern only got stronger with more shots on record. Holding an angle and letting the enemy walk into it plays to your actual strengths; pushing into buildings plays against them.

  3. On Karakin, finish the job

    Best top-10 rate of any map (60%, now on 25 games) but still zero wins. The final circles on a 2×2km map are too small to hide in — treat top-4–5 there as the moment to fight, not the moment to turtle.

  4. Taego has quietly become your problem map, not just Sanhok

    Sanhok is still the worst by average placement, but Taego now has the worst top-10 rate (17.4%) on a real sample (23 games). Different map, different shape of fight — worth paying attention to specifically next time it comes up, since the "why" isn't obvious yet.

  5. You're sharper at the end of a session than the start

    Across every 6+ game session combined, the last third averages a 21.0 placement against 25.7 for the first third. That's the opposite of fatigue — treat the first game or two of a sitting as a warm-up, not a data point.
