POSITIONAL DEEP-DIVE · 564 RECONSTRUCTED FIGHTS

The Engagement Report

Every shot, knock and kill from 213 matches, put back on the map with the circle, the terrain and both of your positions attached. The question isn't how many fights you win — it's which fights, and what they have in common.

D282 & S821 · Duo FPP · 2 Jun – 25 Aug 2026

The Debrief looked at outcomes — placements, K/D, maps. This one looks at mechanism. Every combat event was pulled from the raw match telemetry, grouped into discrete fights, and tagged with where you were standing: how far from the circle centre, inside a town or out in the open, how far apart the two of you were, and who landed the first hit.

The headline is uncomfortable and also the most useful thing in here: you lose most of your fights, and the reason is almost entirely about who starts them. Everything else — guns, maps, terrain — is a rounding error next to that one variable.

Getting shot first is a 4.5× swing, and it happens to you in most fights. When you land the first hit you win 31.8% outright. When they do, you win 7.0%. The damage tells the same story from the other side: opening a fight, you deal 167 and take 140. Getting opened on, you deal 72 and take 182 — you barely get to shoot back. And 78% of ambushed fights end with one of you dead, against 57% of the ones you start.

Worth being precise about what "first hit" means: PUBG only logs shots that connect, so this is who lands the first hit, not who pulls the trigger first. That actually makes the gap more meaningful — it's not about reaction time, it's about who had a firing solution set up before the fight started.

One encouraging note: your first-hit rate climbs through the game — 39% in the first five minutes, 50% in the endgame past 18 minutes. When the map is small and you're deliberately holding a position, you're a different team than you are in the looting phase.

05

Circle Position: Not Edge, Not Deep

For every fight, how far you were from the safe-zone centre as a fraction of its radius. 0 = dead centre, 1.0 = right on the boundary.

Win rate by position within the safe zone. The middle band is clearly best; the rim is clearly worst.

Playing the rim is costing you. Fights in the outer third of the circle (0.65–1.0 of the radius) win at 10.7% — the worst bucket on the board, on a solid n=103. The middle band (0.35–0.65) wins at 26.4%, two and a half times better. Dead centre is mediocre too (17.2%): you end up in the open with people looking in at you from every side.

There's a mechanism behind it, and it's the same one as everywhere else on this page. In the middle band you land the first hit 53.8% of the time — your best number anywhere. On the rim it drops to 38.8%, and outside the zone to 15.8%. Playing the edge means people are rotating through you from behind while your attention is pointed inward, and you keep meeting them on their terms.

A fair warning about this one: across whole matches, average circle position barely predicts placement at all (among games where you survived 15+ minutes, the most-central and most-edge quartiles finish 8.2 and 8.9 on average — effectively identical). So this is a claim about where fights go well, not a claim that hugging the middle all game wins games.

Spacing: The Real Trade-Off

The Debrief corrected the old "spread out" advice, because your simultaneous deaths turned out to be the knockdown rule, not shared grenades. Here is the fuller picture — and it cuts both ways.

Two things that move in opposite directions as you separate: how often you win the fight, and how often a knocked teammate actually gets picked back up.

Separation wins fights and loses teammates. Holding different angles roughly triples your fight win rate — and this survives the obvious objection, because comparing only fights that happened at similar ranges (under 80m), stacked pairs win 12.2% and pairs 75–200m apart win 36.0%. But the revive numbers run the other way and they run hard: inside 25m you pick each other up 36.8% of the time; between 25 and 75m that collapses to 12.6%, and past 75m to 8.3%. Across 287 knockdowns, only 27% ended in a pickup.

At match level, the tight games are the good games: your closest-playing quarter of matches averages 17.3 placement with a 46% top-10 rate, the most spread-out quarter averages 28.4 and 15.7%. So the answer isn't "spread out" — it's that separation is a thing you switch on for a fight and switch off the moment it goes wrong.

The practical shape: hold 30–60m and different angles when you're set up and expecting contact — far enough that one spray or one grenade can't catch both, close enough to close the gap in a few seconds. The instant one of you goes down, the other's job changes from winning the fight to breaking line-of-sight and reaching the body. Right now three-quarters of your knockdowns never get picked up, and that's the step that turns one mistake into a wiped team.

One in five fights you win is followed by another fight inside two minutes, and you lose almost all of them. The sample is small (13 follow-ups), so treat the 85% as a warning rather than a precise figure — but the direction matches the multi-team numbers, where fights against two teams win at 15.3% and three-or-more at 11.1%, against 18.6% for a clean 1v1 duel.

The looting window is the trap. A finished fight leaves you low, out of position and audible from a long way off, and the boxes are usually in the open where the fight happened. The data can't tell you what you were doing in those two minutes, but it can tell you the outcome, and the outcome says the loot is not worth the exposure unless you've moved first.

Things To Actually Try

Ranked by how much the data backs them and how easy they are to just do. Each one is a testable change — play ten games with it and the numbers on this site will tell you whether it worked.

Highest confidence · n=22

1. Stop dropping Bootcamp

22 of 27 Sanhok games, 64% dead inside five minutes, average finish 32.8. This single drop is most of why Sanhok is your worst map.

Do: on Sanhok, pick anything on the outer ring — Ha Tinh, Kampong, Camp Charlie, or just an unnamed cluster near the plane's exit. Same for Gatka on Erangel (70% early deaths) and Pecado on Miramar (45%).

Highest confidence · n=210

2. Fly further from the plane line

Furthest third of your drops: 15.8 average finish, 13% early deaths. Nearest third: 26.8 and 36%. Consistent across every map.

Do: target roughly 450m+ of glide instead of your current ~330m median. Practically: hold the dive longer before opening the chute, and pick a spot one ridge past the obvious one.

Strong · n=113

3. Refuse the fights you didn't start

Ambushed inside 30m you win 7.1% of the time, and that's your single most common situation (113 fights). You are not going to shoot your way out of it at a better rate — the number has 113 games behind it.

Do: treat "we're taking fire and I don't know from where" as an automatic break contact call, not a fight. Smoke, wall, leave. Re-engage on your terms or don't. The 7.1% is what trading anyway looks like.

Strong · n=287 knockdowns

4. Change what happens after a knock

Only 27% of your knockdowns end in a revive. Past 25m apart it drops to 12.6%. And per the Debrief, an un-revived teammate is auto-eliminated the moment the other dies — so a failed pickup costs two lives, not one.

Do: when one goes down, the standing player's job stops being the fight. Break line of sight first, then res — and if the gap is more than about 25–30m with an enemy holding the angle, back off and reset rather than feeding the second body.

Strong · n=273 fights

5. Fight at 30–60m apart, not stacked

At matched fight ranges, stacked pairs (<25m) win 12.2% and separated pairs win 36.0%. But revives collapse past 25m — so the win is in the middle, not at the extremes.

Do: when you're set and expecting contact, hold different angles about 30–60m apart — two firing lines, not one silhouette. Collapse back together the second it goes wrong. You're currently under 25m in 57% of fights.

Solid · n=45

6. D282: bench the M249 and the AK-M

M249 — 31 fights led, 9.7% won. AK-M — 14 fights, 0 won. Together that's 45 fights and 3 wins. The Mini 14 (33.3%) and HK416 (23.8%) are far better in the same hands.

Do: D282 takes Mini 14 / HK416 as the primary pair and leaves the LMG on the floor. If an LMG gets picked up, treat it as a vehicle/suppression tool, not the gun you take into a building.

Solid · n=144

7. S821 takes the marksman slot

S821 wins 66.7% of Mini 14 fights and 45.5% of HK416 fights, at longer median ranges (109m and 25m), and 27.8% of fights overall against D282's 16.2% on identical damage.

Do: when a single good DMR drops, it goes to S821. Pair that with D282 holding the closer angle — that's the split you're already best at, made deliberate.

Moderate · n=103

8. Stop holding the rim

Rim fights (outer third of the circle) win at 10.7%; middle-band fights win at 26.4%. On the rim you land the first hit only 38.8% of the time versus 53.8% in the middle band — people rotate through you from behind.

Do: rotate to sit inside the middle band of the circle rather than shading to the edge, and when you do hold an edge, hold it facing outward at the people still coming in.

Moderate · n=13

9. Move before you loot a kill

21% of won fights are followed by another inside two minutes, and 85% of those follow-ups are losses. Small sample, but it matches the multi-team numbers.

Do: after a won fight, reposition first, loot second — or skip the box entirely if you're already kitted. Assume someone heard it.

No signal — don't bother

10. Who drives: leave it alone

You asked specifically about this. D282 drives in 52 matches, S821 in 29, and once you compare only games that reached 15 minutes there's no meaningful placement difference (8.5 vs 10.6, with n=38 and n=20). The big raw gap between "driving" and "no driving" games is just survival time — dead players don't drive.

Method & Caveats

So you can tell how much weight each number deserves.

  • Source: raw PUBG match telemetry for 213 matches (2 Jun – 25 Aug 2026) — every position ping, damage, knock and kill, not the summary stats the other pages use.
  • A "fight" is a cluster of combat events involving either player, split whenever 45+ seconds pass with no combat. 564 fights against humans; 20 bot-only fights were excluded from every headline number (you win 60% of those, which would flatter the totals).
  • "Win" = you knocked or killed someone and neither of you went down. "Trade" = both sides lost someone. "Loss" = only your side. 179 fights ended with nobody downed and are excluded from win-rate figures.
  • "First hit" is who landed the first damaging hit — PUBG doesn't log misses, so this is not literally who pulled the trigger first.
  • Terrain uses PUBG's named-location tag. Unnamed farm buildings count as "open", so the compound bucket means "inside a named town", not "inside any structure".
  • Circle position is distance from safe-zone centre divided by its radius, sampled at the midpoint of each fight.
  • Match-level claims are confounded by survival time — dying early prevents driving, rotating and late fights. Where it mattered, figures are also shown for games that reached 15 minutes, and that's flagged in the text.
  • Correlation, not proof. These are patterns in 213 games of your own play, not controlled experiments. The point of Chapter 10 is that they're cheap to test.
